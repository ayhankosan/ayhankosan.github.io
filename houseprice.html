Six Decades of US Home Values: State Medians from the Census, 1940–2000

Category Data Visualization, Web-Mapping, JavaScript
Date 2025

Median home value by state, 1940–2000

Every decennial census since 1940 has asked homeowners what their house is worth. This map shows the state medians of those answers, adjusted for inflation to constant 2000 dollars, so the decades compare directly. Nationally the median value of a single-family home nearly quadrupled, from $30,600 in 1940 to $119,600 in 2000.

Switch between Value, which shows the dollar amounts on one fixed scale for every decade, and Compared with US median, which shows each state as a share of the national median for that year. Use the slider or Play to step through the decades. Hover over a state for its figures, and select one to see its full history below the map.

About the data

Measure
Median value of owner-occupied single-family homes on less than 10 acres, with no business or medical office on the property. Half the homes in a state were worth more and half less.
What "value" means
The value is the owner's own estimate of what the house and lot would sell for, given on the census questionnaire. It is not a recorded sale price, and it covers all owned homes, not only those that sold that year.
Dollars
Every figure is in constant 2000 dollars, adjusted for inflation by the Census Bureau. A rising line means home values grew faster than general prices.
Coverage
50 states and the District of Columbia, which the Census Bureau treats as a state in these tables. Alaska and Hawaii have no 1940 value: both were territories and not part of the 1940 state tables. Puerto Rico is not included.
Color classes
Value uses the same eight fixed classes for every decade, so a color means the same dollar range in 1940 as in 2000. Compared with US median divides each state's value by that year's national median; values near 100% are white, below the median are blue and above it are red.
Caveats
Owner estimates tend to lag the market when prices turn. The 1990 figures caught the end of a late-1980s boom in the Northeast and California, which is why several of those states show lower values in 2000 than in 1990. The basemap uses Web Mercator, which enlarges northern states, so judge states by color and not by size.

Technical Context

  • Leaflet with the muted Esri Light Gray Canvas basemap; place labels sit on their own pane above the state colors so they stay readable
  • Fixed, manually defined class breaks with a ColorBrewer yellow–orange–red ramp, so colors stay comparable across all seven censuses
  • A second, diverging blue–red view normalized to the national median for each year, which takes out nationwide growth and shows the relative position of each state
  • Legend built from the same break definitions as the map, with a count of states per class for the selected year
  • D3.js line chart of the selected state against the national median, with rank, share of median and decade change computed in the browser
  • Keyboard-accessible controls and a ranked data table as a non-visual alternative to the map

Some regional patterns: the District of Columbia had the highest median value in 1940 and 1950, and Hawaii from 1960 on. Arkansas, Mississippi and Oklahoma were among the lowest throughout. Western states such as Arizona, Nevada and Utah moved from below the national median in 1940 to above it by 2000.
