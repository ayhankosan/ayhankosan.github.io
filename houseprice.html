Eight Decades of US Home Values: State Medians, 1940–2025

Category Data Visualization, Web-Mapping, JavaScript
Date 2025

Median home value by state, 1940–2025

Since 1940 the Census Bureau has asked homeowners what their house is worth: in every decennial census through 2000, and every year since in the American Community Survey (ACS). This map shows the state medians of those answers from 1940 to 2025, all converted to 2025 dollars so the years compare directly. After inflation, the national median home value rose from about $57,000 in 1940 to $371,100 in 2025, roughly six and a half times as much.

Switch between Value, which shows the dollar amounts on one fixed scale for every decade, and Compared with US median, which shows each state as a share of the national median for that year. Use the slider or Play to step through the years. Hover over a state for its figures, and select one to see its full history below the map.

About the data

Measure
The median value of owner-occupied homes in each state: half the homes were worth more, half less. The value is the owner's own estimate of what the house and lot would sell for, not a recorded sale price, and it covers all owned homes, not only those that sold that year.
1940–2000
Decennial census, from the Census Bureau's Historical Census of Housing Tables. These cover owner-occupied single-family homes on less than 10 acres with no business or medical office on the property.
2010–2025
American Community Survey, table B25077, covering all owner-occupied homes, condominiums and mobile homes included. 2010 and 2025 are 1-year estimates. 2020 uses the 2016–2020 5-year estimates, because the Census Bureau did not release standard 1-year estimates for 2020 after pandemic disruption to data collection.
Change of definition
Because the ACS covers more kinds of homes, its medians run slightly lower than the single-family series. Census 2000 published both: $119,600 for single-family homes and $111,800 for all owned homes. The chart shows the all-homes 2000 value as a hollow square, and changes into 2010 are measured from it, so they compare like with like. Comparisons across the full 1940–2025 span include this switch.
Dollars
Every figure is in constant 2025 dollars. The Census Bureau published the 1940–2000 values in 2000 dollars; these and the ACS values are converted to 2025 dollars with BLS CPI-U annual averages (2000: 172.2, 2010: 218.056, 2020: 258.811, 2025: 321.943). Values are rounded to the nearest $100. A rising line means home values grew faster than general prices.
Sampling error
ACS figures come from a sample survey; the state panel shows each one's 90% margin of error. For most states it is under 2% of the value, so differences of a few thousand dollars between neighboring ranks are not meaningful.
Coverage
50 states and the District of Columbia, which the Census Bureau treats as a state in these tables. Alaska and Hawaii have no 1940 value: both were territories and not part of the 1940 state tables. Puerto Rico is not included.
Color classes
Value uses the same eight fixed classes for every year, so a color means the same dollar range in 1940 as in 2025. Compared with US median divides each state's value by that year's national median, which also cancels out the change of definition; values near 100% are white, below the median are blue and above it are red.
Caveats
Owner estimates tend to lag the market when prices turn. The 1990 figures caught the end of a late-1980s boom in the Northeast and California, which is why several of those states show lower values in 2000 than in 1990. The basemap uses Web Mercator, which enlarges northern states, so judge states by color and not by size.

Technical Context

  • Leaflet with the muted Esri Light Gray Canvas basemap; place labels sit on their own pane above the state colors so they stay readable
  • Two Census Bureau sources joined into one 1940–2025 series: decennial census tables to 2000 and American Community Survey table B25077 from 2010, all converted to 2025 dollars with BLS CPI-U, and a bridge value at 2000 that keeps the change of definition visible
  • Fixed, manually defined class breaks in 2025 dollars with a ColorBrewer yellow–orange–red ramp, so colors stay comparable across all ten years
  • A second, diverging blue–red view normalized to the national median for each year, which takes out nationwide growth and shows the relative position of each state
  • Legend built from the same break definitions as the map, with a count of states per class for the selected year
  • D3.js line chart of the selected state against the national median, with rank, share of median, change and ACS margins of error computed in the browser
  • Keyboard-accessible controls and a ranked data table as a non-visual alternative to the map

Some regional patterns: the District of Columbia had the highest median value in 1940 and 1950, and Hawaii from 1960 on. Arkansas, Mississippi and Oklahoma were among the lowest throughout. Western states such as Arizona, Nevada and Utah moved from below the national median in 1940 to above it by 2000, and Montana and Idaho followed between 2000 and 2020.
